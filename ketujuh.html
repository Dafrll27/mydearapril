#MeToo Movement

Do you know about the #MeToo movement?
If not, you should read this!

How #MeToo Began

In 2006, in the United States, an activist named Tarana Burke began using the phrase “Me Too.” Tarana worked with young women, especially survivors of sexual violence from vulnerable communities. She realized that the issue was not only the number of cases, but also how these survivors felt alone, as if their experiences were isolated, not part of a bigger problem. “Me Too” was born as a form of empathy and solidarity, a way of saying “I’ve been through that too, and you are not alone.” At that stage, the movement was not widely known, not viral, and not yet a global conversation. It existed only in small spaces, as a form of support between individuals trying to understand each other’s experiences.

However, more than a decade later, with the same social conditions, a culture of silence, unequal power relations, and the normalization of inappropriate behavior, it finally reached a breaking point. In 2017, in the Hollywood entertainment industry, the case of Harvey Weinstein, an influential film producer, was exposed to the public. Journalistic investigations revealed that for years, he allegedly used his position to commit sexual harassment and violence against many women. Dozens of victims began to speak out, showing that this was not an isolated case, but a systematic pattern. This case became a symbol of long-standing injustice. The perpetrator was protected by power, while the victims were silenced by fear and by a system that did not stand with them.

In that moment, an actress named Alyssa Milano encouraged people through social media to share their experiences using the hashtag #MeToo. The invitation was simple, but the impact was extraordinary. In a short time, millions of people from different countries, backgrounds, and professions began using the hashtag to tell their stories. From there, “Me Too”, which was originally an expression of empathy, transformed into a global social movement.

The movement then evolved into a collective space to reveal what had long been hidden. It does not only talk about extreme forms of sexual violence, but also opens discussions about a wider spectrum, including degrading comments, inappropriate jokes, and situations that make someone feel unsafe. #MeToo shows that injustice does not only lie in individual actions, but also in the system that supports them, such as victim-blaming culture, unequal power relations, and the normalization of behaviors that should never be considered acceptable.

Even so, the movement is not without criticism. Not all victims have equal space to be heard, and not everyone who speaks out receives protection. Some even face serious social and professional consequences. This shows that although #MeToo has opened a global conversation, structural change remains a challenge that is not yet fully resolved.

In the end, #MeToo is not only about who speaks, where, or when. More than that, it is about how personal experiences that were once hidden finally find a space to be collectively acknowledged. It turns something that was once seen as an “individual case” into an awareness that this is a systemic issue that requires shared attention.

My #Metoo

Understanding #MeToo often makes us think that these stories only happen somewhere “far away,” in big industries, among people with high power. As if it is not part of everyday life. Until we realize, the story might not be that far. It could even be something we have experienced ourselves, without realizing it.

What I Saw and What I Felt

If someone says that childhood is always associated with innocence, maybe you need to read about my experience in my environment. Honestly, my earliest memory of elementary school is not about learning to read or playing during breaks, but about the shock of seeing how children that age, especially boys, were already talking about things far beyond their age, not just vaguely knowing, but reaching a level that was clearly inappropriate, both in words and actions. Meanwhile, I was only equipped with basic education about body boundaries and self-protection, which felt very different from what I saw around me, because my friends were not just talking, they were imitating, trying, acting in ways that were not even supposed to be understood at that age. The question is, where did they learn all of that from?

We are often too quick to blame “the children,” as if they suddenly appeared with that kind of behavior. But if we look deeper, this is not just about individuals, but about an environment that allows it and even contributes to it. Because at the same time, I also saw some teachers, figures who should be the last boundary between children and a world they are not ready for, joking about sexual things, sometimes between male and female teachers, sometimes in front of students, as if it was something light, as if there were no consequences, as if the children in front of them were not absorbing everything without any filter. At that point, the line between what is “acceptable” and “not acceptable” was not just blurred, it was completely erased.

Moving to the next level of education, my expectations were slightly higher, especially because the school I chose carried a “religiously integrated” label, which logically should have stronger values, clearer boundaries, and more awareness of what is appropriate and not. However, the reality felt like a more explicit version of what I had already seen before, where the language became more vulgar, the illustrations more explicit, and what was most disturbing was not only the behavior of the students, but the fact that some educators were also involved in inappropriate actions or jokes. Strangely, this was never truly questioned, it was often considered normal. This is a strong irony, considering that an institution carrying the name of “religion” fails to protect the most basic thing: a sense of safety and moral boundaries.

Then, moving to the next level of education, I might not have had much space to observe because of the COVID-19 pandemic, but even within those limitations, the same pattern still appeared. There were still individuals involved, the behavior kept repeating, and again, mostly dominated by males, as if this was no longer a coincidence, but a pattern that continues without ever truly being broken.

There was one moment that perhaps best illustrates how broken this system is. It happened when I was not even 12 years old yet, at a school promotion event in a religious-based school, which confidently invited a religious figure, someone who symbolically should be a guardian of values, a representation of morality, and an example of ethics. But what happened was the opposite. He made jokes about adult relationships in front of children who were not even old enough to understand the context. What was even more shocking was not only what he said, but the reaction around him, parents laughed, teachers stayed silent, no one stopped him, no one corrected him, as if everyone agreed that it was “normal,” “funny,” and “not a problem.”

At that point, what I felt was not just disagreement, but deep discomfort. As a child, I was placed in a space where boundaries were collectively violated, and what was even more confusing, there was not a single adult acting as protection.

If this had only happened once, maybe it could be called an “isolated case.” But the reality is, it kept happening, at school, in the environment, even in some religious discussions or events in my area, where inappropriate jokes continued to appear and continued to be laughed at, as if it had become part of a shared culture.

So the problem is not just one or two individuals, but something deeper and more complex. A social system that has been flawed from the beginning, where the environment allows it, authority figures set the wrong example, and society chooses to normalize instead of correcting it. We talk about morality, but in practice, we contradict it. We claim to uphold values, but in everyday life, we slowly destroy them.

Something that is clearly inappropriate, clearly uncomfortable, clearly beyond age boundaries, can continue to exist and repeat itself not because people don’t know, but because too many choose to treat it as normal.

Sexual Violence on Indonesian Campuses

Then I continued my education to the next level…

In recent times, educational spaces in Indonesia, which should be the safest place to learn and grow, have once again been highlighted due to cases that show the same pattern: sexual violence, power relations, and normalization that is difficult to break.

At Universitas Indonesia, for example, the public was shocked by a case of alleged verbal sexual harassment involving several students in the Faculty of Law. The case began in a digital space, something often considered trivial, but later developed into a serious issue handled by a special task force. Sixteen students were examined in an ongoing investigation, with an approach that emphasizes victim protection and fairness for all parties. What is interesting is that this case shows that violence does not always come in physical form. It can appear in words, in conversations, in spaces considered “normal,” but the impact remains real.

On the other hand, at IPB University, a similar case emerged from conversations in a student WhatsApp group. It initially circulated as “batch news,” but gradually unfolded into alleged sexual harassment involving many parties, even student organization officials. More than just the content of the conversation, what became the focus was how the case dragged on without clarity, triggering anger among students who then organized and demanded transparency. Here, the issue is not only the act itself, but also the system, how slow and unclear responses prolong the sense of insecurity for victims.

A similar situation occurred at Institut Teknologi Bandung, when an old song performed in a student activity received criticism for containing elements of verbal sexual harassment. The song was considered a “tradition,” something that had existed for a long time and was continuously maintained without being questioned. Until finally, people began to ask, since when did something degrading become a tradition? This case reveals an important point: normalization often comes from habits that are never evaluated.

Even at Universitas Padjadjaran, alleged sexual harassment involved someone in a high academic position, showing that power relations do not only occur among students, but also within formal structures. When the perpetrator is in a position of authority, the space for victims to speak becomes even more limited.

At first glance, these cases may seem different, some happen in digital spaces, some within organizational culture, some involve individuals with high positions. But if we look deeper, they are all connected by the same pattern: unequal power, slow institutional response, and a culture that often considers these issues as something not worth making a big deal of. The irony is that all of this happens in spaces that are supposed to teach values, ethics, and awareness.

A Personal Note

As an important note, I want to make this clear to avoid misunderstanding. Everything I have shared above is not an attempt to discredit the institutions where I studied. On the contrary, these schools have played a significant role in shaping who I am today, both academically and personally. I am truly grateful to everyone involved, the dedicated teachers, the environment that allowed me to grow, and the many meaningful experiences I gained.

These pieces of reality happened from around 2009 to 2026, reflecting the social conditions I experienced during those years. In the early years, around 2009, society might not have had enough awareness regarding boundaries, ethics, and sensitivity toward inappropriate behavior, especially in educational environments. So this is not about blaming one side or generalizing, but about acknowledging that there were gaps that were once considered normal.

Now, awareness of this issue is increasingly being raised everywhere, whether about child protection, educator ethics, or communication boundaries. The hope is not to bring up the past for its own sake, but so that all educational institutions, including those I attended, can become better, wiser, and more firm in upholding the values and morals they are meant to represent.
