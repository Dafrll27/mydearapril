When Humans Lose Everything, What Is Still Left?

When Humans Lose Everything, What Is Still Left?

There are books that, once finished, slowly fade from memory. We close the pages, put them back on the shelf, and life continues as usual. But there are also books that only begin to work after the last page is read. They stay in our minds, slip into the way we see life and quietly change something inside us.

Man's Search for Meaning by Viktor E. Frankl

This book is not just a memoir about Nazi concentration camps. It is also not a motivational book that offers cheap optimism about how life will always be okay. Instead, this book was born from the ruins of a man’s life who had seen the world in its most brutal form. Frankl was a Jewish psychiatrist from Austria who survived Auschwitz, a place where humans were no longer treated as humans but as numbers that could be erased at any time. He lost his family, his freedom, his identity and almost lost his reason to live. Yet from the darkest place, he discovered one of the most important ideas in life, that humans can still find meaning, even in the most inhumane suffering.

Maybe that is why this book still feels so relevant today. Because quietly, many modern people are also living through different forms of suffering. Not everyone is in a concentration camp but many people live with pressure, loneliness, anxiety, emptiness and a loss of direction. Many look perfectly fine on social media but secretly feel hollow inside. Many are busy chasing careers, validation or success, yet do not truly know why they are doing all of it.

Frankl’s question feels even more terrifying:
What actually makes humans still want to live?

There is one thing people often misunderstand about suffering. We think the most painful thing in life is the pain itself. But often, what destroys people the most is when suffering feels meaningless.

Someone can survive working day and night if they know who they are fighting for. A mother can endure incredible exhaustion for her child. Someone who is seriously ill can still smile because they believe there is still a reason to keep living. But when hope disappears, humans slowly collapse along with it.

Frankl saw this directly in Auschwitz. He witnessed how prisoners who lost the meaning of life usually became the first people to give up on death. They were not always the physically weakest, but they were the ones who had stopped seeing a future. When someone no longer has a “why” to live, then the “how” of life becomes too heavy to carry. In this book, Frankl quotes Friedrich Nietzsche:

“He who has a why to live can bear almost any how.”

The sentence sounds simple, but after reading the whole book, it feels like something carved from the deepest human suffering.

Frankl does not write suffering as something beautiful. He is not romanticizing wounds. Instead, he shows how cruel life was in the concentration camps, starvation, forced labor, humiliation, violence, fear of the gas chambers and the process of dehumanization that slowly stripped people of their identity. Prisoners were forced to give up all their belongings, have their heads shaved, receive numbers and be treated like objects.

There is a very painful part when Frankl describes the selection process in Auschwitz. An SS soldier would simply point to the right or left. Those sent to the right could still live and work. Those sent to the left were sent to the gas chambers. Human life was decided that easily. And what is terrifying is that, over time, people slowly became used to the horror.

This is where the book becomes more than just a historical record. Frankl is dissecting human psychology. He shows how humans can change when they continuously live in suffering. In the beginning, prisoners experienced extreme shock and fear. But after some time, a phase of apathy appeared, a condition where emotions slowly died. Prisoners began seeing violence without feeling surprised anymore. They became numb to suffering.

Isn’t that something that also happens in modern life?

We live in a time when people see suffering so often that they eventually lose sensitivity. News about war passes by casually on phone screens. Violence becomes daily entertainment. People can see someone mentally falling apart and simply say, “Everyone is tired too.” We become quicker to judge, yet slower to understand.

Slowly, the modern world is also creating emotionally numb people.

In the second part of the book, Frankl introduces logotherapy, a psychological approach centered on the search for meaning in life. He believed that the main human drive is not pleasure, as Sigmund Freud believed, nor power, as in Alfred Adler’s ideas, but rather the need to find meaning.

According to Frankl, humans can find meaning through three things, meaningful work or actions, love and the courage to face suffering.

Interestingly, love in this book is not described in an overly romantic way. In the middle of the concentration camp, Frankl survived by imagining his wife. He realized that even though the human body can be imprisoned, the inner world remains free. Even in the most horrifying conditions, humans can still love, remember and hope.

We are too busy chasing big things that we forget that the meaning of life is often hidden in small places, conversations with people we love, the feeling of enoughness, the courage to endure, or the ability to remain a good person in a harsh world.

Frankl also wrote something deeply relevant to today’s world when he said that happiness and success cannot be chased directly. The more obsessively someone chases happiness, the emptier they feel. Happiness comes as a side effect when humans dedicate themselves to something bigger than themselves.

Isn’t it true that many people today are exhausted because they keep chasing definitions of success that are not even truly theirs?

We live in an era where productivity is treated as identity. People are forced to keep moving, keep growing, keep succeeding. As if human value is determined only by achievement. Even though humans are not machines.

This book feels like a reminder that life is not just about surviving or winning. Life is about finding reasons why we still want to wake up every morning.

In the end, Man’s Search for Meaning is not only a book about the Holocaust. It is a book about humanity. About how fragile humans become when they lose hope, but also about how strong humans become when they find meaning.

Frankl shows both sides of humanity at once. Humans who created gas chambers and humans who still walked toward death while carrying prayers on their lips.

That sentence may become one of the most reflective realizations, that within all of us, there is always the possibility of becoming cruel or compassionate. That the world is not only built by large systems, but also by the small choices humans make every day.

If one day life takes away everything we are proud of, such as work, status, relationships, comfort and everything else until nothing is left of us,

Would we still know our reason for living?

Did Frankl, who always tried to remember his wife in the middle of suffering, finally meet his wife again in the end? If you’re curious, just read the book yourself… bye.
